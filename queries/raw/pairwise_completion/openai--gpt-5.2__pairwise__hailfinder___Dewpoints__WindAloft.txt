1. **Interpret the variables**
   - **Dewpoint** is a measure of near-surface moisture content (linked to evaporation, moisture sources, and especially **moisture advection**).
   - **WindAloft** describes winds in the free atmosphere (driven mainly by **pressure/height gradients**, jet stream dynamics, and synoptic-scale patterns).

2. **Check physical plausibility of A (Dewpoints → WindAloft)**
   - Changing dewpoints (moisture) can affect buoyancy and convection locally, but it typically **does not directly set the large-scale pressure gradients aloft** that control wind aloft.
   - Any pathway from dewpoints to winds aloft would be indirect and conditional (e.g., convection altering upper-level flow), not the usual or dominant direction.

3. **Check physical plausibility of B (WindAloft → Dewpoints)**
   - Winds aloft are tightly connected to synoptic setups that also control **airmass transport**.
   - Wind patterns (including low-level jet and broader flow) frequently **advect moisture** from source regions (e.g., Gulf moisture) into an area, raising dewpoints, or advect drier air in, lowering them.
   - Even though “dewpoint” is near-surface, the larger circulation (represented by winds aloft) often serves as a **cause** of moisture changes via transport and mixing.

4. **Most likely causal direction**
   - In operational meteorology and hail forecasting contexts, it’s more common that **the atmospheric flow (winds) helps determine moisture (dewpoints)** through advection, rather than dewpoints determining winds aloft.

<Answer>B</Answer>
