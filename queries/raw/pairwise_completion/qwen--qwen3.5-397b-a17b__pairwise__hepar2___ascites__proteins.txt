To determine the correct cause-and-effect relationship, let's analyze the medical pathophysiology involved with ascites and proteins.

1.  **Define Ascites:** Ascites is the abnormal accumulation of fluid in the peritoneal cavity (the abdomen). It is most commonly associated with liver cirrhosis, but can also occur in heart failure, kidney failure, and certain cancers.

2.  **Define Proteins in this Context:** In the context of fluid balance and ascites, "proteins" refers primarily to serum albumin, a protein produced by the liver. Albumin is crucial for maintaining **oncotic pressure** (colloid osmotic pressure), which helps keep fluid inside the blood vessels.

3.  **Analyze the Mechanism (Proteins $\rightarrow$ Ascites):**
    *   When the liver is damaged (e.g., cirrhosis), its ability to synthesize albumin decreases, leading to **hypoalbuminemia** (low protein levels in the blood).
    *   Low serum protein levels reduce the oncotic pressure within the blood vessels.
    *   Without sufficient oncotic pressure to counteract hydrostatic pressure, fluid leaks out of the blood vessels and into the surrounding tissues and body cavities.
    *   This leakage into the abdominal cavity results in **ascites**.
    *   Therefore, low protein levels are a direct contributing cause to the development of ascites.

4.  **Analyze the Reverse Mechanism (Ascites $\rightarrow$ Proteins):**
    *   While protein can be lost *into* the ascitic fluid once it forms, the presence of ascites itself does not cause the systemic production or regulation of proteins. The low protein level is typically a precursor or a concurrent result of the underlying disease (like liver failure) that drives the ascites, rather than the ascites causing the protein status.

5.  **Conclusion:** The causal flow is that abnormal protein levels (specifically low albumin) contribute to the physiological conditions that create ascites.
