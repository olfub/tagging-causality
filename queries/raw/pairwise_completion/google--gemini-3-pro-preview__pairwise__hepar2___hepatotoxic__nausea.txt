To determine the most likely cause-and-effect relationship between "hepatotoxic" and "nausea," let's analyze the medical definitions and physiological pathways of both terms.

**Step 1: Define the terms.**
*   **Hepatotoxic:** This adjective refers to a substance, drug, or chemical that is damaging or destructive to liver cells (hepatocytes). Hepatotoxicity is the state of liver injury.
*   **Nausea:** This is a symptom; a sensation of unease and discomfort in the upper stomach with an involuntary urge to vomit.

**Step 2: Analyze Relationship A (Hepatotoxic causes nausea).**
*   When the liver is injured by a hepatotoxic substance (e.g., acetaminophen overdose, alcohol, certain antibiotics), the liver's ability to filter toxins from the blood is compromised.
*   Furthermore, hepatotoxicity triggers an inflammatory response (hepatitis).
*   The accumulation of toxins (such as ammonia or bilirubin) and the release of inflammatory cytokines are well-documented triggers for the chemoreceptor trigger zone (CTZ) in the brainstem.
*   Stimulation of the CTZ results in the sensation of nausea.
*   *Clinical Evidence:* Nausea is a classic, early prodromal symptom of drug-induced liver injury (DILI) and acute liver failure.

**Step 3: Analyze Relationship B (Nausea causes hepatotoxic).**
*   Nausea is a neurological and gastric sensation. While severe retching or vomiting *might* cause physical strain (like a Mallory-Weiss tear in the esophagus), the sensation of nausea itself does not chemically destroy liver cells.
*   There is no physiological mechanism by which the sensation of nausea induces toxicity in the liver. Nausea is a symptom, not a toxic agent.

**Step 4: Conclusion.**
Hepatotoxicity (liver damage) leads to systemic physiological changes that produce nausea as a symptom. Nausea, conversely, does not cause the liver to become toxic. Therefore, the causal direction flows from the liver injury to the symptom.
