This is a fundamental question of causality in medicine. To determine the correct relationship, let's break down the concepts of "Disease" and being "Sick" (illness).

**1. Defining the Terms:**
*   **Disease:** A disease is a specific structural or functional disorder in the body. In the context of pediatric congenital heart disease, this refers to the actual anatomical defect (e.g., a Ventricular Septal Defect or Tetralogy of Fallot). This is the biological reality or pathology.
*   **Sick:** Being "sick" generally refers to the subjective experience of feeling unwell or the manifestation of symptoms (illness). This includes clinical signs like cyanosis (turning blue), fatigue, poor feeding, or rapid breathing.

**2. The Mechanism of Action:**
*   **Scenario A (Disease causes Sick):** A structural defect in the heart (the disease) alters blood flow. This altered physiology leads to low oxygen levels or fluid overload. These physiological changes result in symptoms perceived by the patient or observed by the doctor (the state of being sick). This follows the standard medical model: Pathology $\rightarrow$ Pathophysiology $\rightarrow$ Clinical Presentation.
*   **Scenario B (Sick causes Disease):** This suggests that the feeling of being unwell or the presence of symptoms spontaneously generates a structural heart defect. While chronic stress on the body can lead to *new* health issues, feeling "sick" does not retroactively create a congenital anatomical defect that was present at birth.

**3. Application to Congenital Heart Disease:**
*   A child is born with a heart defect (the disease).
*   Because of that defect, the child experiences symptoms like failure to thrive or breathlessness (the sickness).
*   Therefore, the presence of the disease is the root cause of the state of sickness.

**4. Conclusion:**
The disease is the underlying biological cause, and being "sick" is the effect or the manifestation of that disease. Treating the "sickness" (symptoms) without addressing the "disease" usually does not cure the patient, whereas treating the "disease" (e.g., through surgery) often eliminates the "sickness."
