To determine the more likely cause-and-effect relationship between dewpoints and wind aloft, let's analyze each component step by step:

1. **Understanding Dewpoints**: Dewpoint is a measure of the temperature at which the air becomes saturated with moisture and dew or frost begins to form. High dewpoints indicate high levels of moisture in the air.

2. **Understanding Wind Aloft**: Wind aloft refers to the winds that occur at higher altitudes in the atmosphere, typically above 2,000 to 5,000 feet. These winds can influence weather patterns significantly, including the movement and development of storms.

3. **Analyzing the Relationship**:
   - **Dewpoints causing Wind Aloft (A)**: While dewpoints can influence local weather conditions, such as fog formation or contributing to the development of thunderstorms, they are more of a surface-level condition. The direct influence of surface dewpoints on wind patterns at higher altitudes (wind aloft) is less straightforward. Generally, dewpoints are indicative of atmospheric moisture content near the surface rather than a direct cause of wind patterns at higher altitudes.
   - **Wind Aloft causing Dewpoints (B)**: Wind aloft can significantly influence the movement of air masses and weather systems, which in turn can affect surface conditions, including dewpoints. For example, wind aloft can bring in air masses with different moisture contents, thereby influencing the dewpoint temperatures at the surface. This relationship suggests that wind patterns at higher altitudes can have a more direct influence on surface dewpoints by transporting air masses with different characteristics.

Considering these points, the relationship where **Wind Aloft causes Dewpoints** seems more plausible because wind aloft can influence the movement of air masses and thus affect the moisture content (and therefore the dewpoint) near the surface. This does not mean dewpoints cannot influence local atmospheric conditions that might indirectly affect wind patterns, but the direct causality from wind aloft to surface conditions like dewpoints appears stronger.

<Answer>B</Answer>
