To determine which cause-and-effect relationship is more likely between "Residence" and "Travel," let's break down the potential relationships step by step.

1. **Understanding the Terms**:
   - **Residence** refers to the place where someone lives or has their home.
   - **Travel** refers to the act of moving from one place to another, typically for business, pleasure, or other purposes.

2. **Analyzing Cause-and-Effect**:
   - A cause-and-effect relationship implies that one event (the cause) leads to another event (the effect).
   - **Residence causes Travel**: This suggests that where a person lives (their residence) influences or leads to their decision to travel. For example, living in a location with easy access to airports or travel hubs might encourage more travel.
   - **Travel causes Residence**: This implies that traveling leads to a change in residence. For instance, someone might travel to a new location and decide to move there, changing their residence.

3. **Logical Reasoning**:
   - Logically, a person's residence (where they currently live) can influence many aspects of their life, including their opportunities, preferences, and decisions about travel. For example, people living in areas with significant cultural or economic ties to another region might travel more to that region.
   - Conversely, traveling can indeed lead to a decision to change one's residence, based on experiences or opportunities encountered during travel. However, travel is more often a consequence of various factors including residence, rather than the primary cause of changing one's residence.

4. **Determining Likelihood**:
   - Considering the complexity of human decision-making and the various factors that influence both travel and residential choices, both directions of causality are possible. However, **Travel causes Residence** seems less common as a direct cause-and-effect relationship compared to **Residence causes Travel**. This is because while travel can certainly influence decisions about where to live, the initial decision to travel often stems from factors related to one's current residence, such as proximity to travel hubs, economic status, or personal networks.

5. **Conclusion**:
   - Based on the above analysis, **Residence causes Travel** appears to be a more common and direct cause-and-effect relationship. This is because one's residence can more directly influence opportunities, resources, and motivations for travel.
