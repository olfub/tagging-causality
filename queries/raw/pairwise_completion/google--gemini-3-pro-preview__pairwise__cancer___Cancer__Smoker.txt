As an assistant to a lung cancer expert, let's break down the causality of these two statements based on medical consensus and biological mechanisms.

**Step 1: Analyze Statement A ("Cancer causes Smoker")**
This statement suggests that developing lung cancer causes a person to begin smoking or become a smoker.
*   **Temporal Sequence:** For a cause to exist, it must precede the effect. In the vast majority of cases, patients have a history of smoking *before* a cancer diagnosis is made.
*   **Plausibility:** While a diagnosis might cause a person to change their habits (often quitting, rarely starting), there is no biological mechanism by which the growth of malignant cells in the lungs compels a person to inhale tobacco smoke. This direction of causality contradicts established medical history taking.

**Step 2: Analyze Statement B ("Smoker causes Cancer")**
This statement suggests that being a smoker leads to the development of lung cancer.
*   **Biological Mechanism:** Tobacco smoke contains over 7,000 chemicals, at least 69 of which are known carcinogens (cancer-causing agents). These chemicals damage the DNA in the cells of the lungs. Over time, this damage causes cells to grow abnormally and uncontrollably, forming tumors.
*   **Epidemiological Evidence:** Extensive research has established a strong correlation where smoking is the number one risk factor for lung cancer. In the United States, cigarette smoking is linked to about 80% to 90% of lung cancer deaths.
*   **Dose-Response Relationship:** There is a clear dose-response relationship; the more heavily and the longer a person smokes, the higher their risk of developing cancer.

**Step 3: Conclusion**
Decades of medical research, clinical studies, and biological evidence prove that the carcinogens introduced by smoking damage lung tissue and lead to malignancy. Therefore, smoking is the cause, and cancer is the potential effect.
