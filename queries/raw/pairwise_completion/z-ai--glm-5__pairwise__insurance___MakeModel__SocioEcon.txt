Let's analyze the relationship between **SocioEcon** (Socioeconomic Status) and **MakeModel** (the make and model of a car).

**Step 1: Define the variables.**
*   **SocioEcon:** This represents a person's socioeconomic standing, typically defined by income, wealth, education, and occupation.
*   **MakeModel:** This represents the specific vehicle a person owns (e.g., a luxury sedan vs. an economy compact car).

**Step 2: Analyze Causal Direction A (MakeModel causes SocioEcon).**
Does owning a specific car *cause* a person to have a specific socioeconomic status?
While owning a luxury car might signal wealth, the car itself is a possession. Buying a car does not inherently generate the income, education, or occupational status that defines socioeconomic class. In fact, one could go into debt to purchase a car they cannot afford, which might contradict their actual socioeconomic status. Therefore, the physical object (the car) does not create the social standing.

**Step 3: Analyze Causal Direction B (SocioEcon causes MakeModel).**
Does a person's socioeconomic status *cause* them to purchase a specific make and model?
This aligns with standard economic principles. A person's income and wealth (SocioEcon) determine their purchasing power. This budget constraint heavily influences whether they buy a new vs. used car, a luxury brand vs. an economy brand, or a specific model suited to their lifestyle and income level. Wealthier individuals (higher SocioEcon) are more likely to own luxury vehicles, while those with lower income are more likely to own budget-friendly vehicles.

**Step 4: Compare the likelihood.**
Direction B represents a fundamental driver of consumer behavior: financial means dictate purchasing decisions. Direction A implies that purchasing an item creates the wealth required to buy it, which reverses the logical order of cause and effect.

<Answer>B</Answer>
