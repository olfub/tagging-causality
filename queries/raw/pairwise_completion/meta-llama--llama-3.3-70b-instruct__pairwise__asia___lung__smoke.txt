To determine the more likely cause-and-effect relationship between lung cancer and smoking, let's analyze the two options step by step.

1. **Understanding the Basics**: First, we need to understand what lung cancer and smoking are. Lung cancer is a type of cancer that affects the lungs, often caused by the uncontrolled growth of abnormal cells in one or both lungs. Smoking refers to the act of inhaling smoke from burning tobacco products, such as cigarettes, cigars, or pipes.

2. **Medical Evidence**: A vast amount of medical research and evidence suggests that there is a strong link between smoking and the development of lung cancer. This evidence comes from epidemiological studies, clinical trials, and basic scientific research into the carcinogenic effects of tobacco smoke.

3. **Causal Mechanism**: The mechanism by which smoking could cause lung cancer involves the inhalation of carcinogens (cancer-causing substances) present in tobacco smoke. These carcinogens can damage the DNA of lung cells, leading to mutations that may result in cancerous growth.

4. **Temporal Relationship**: For a cause-and-effect relationship to be established, the cause must precede the effect in time. In the case of smoking and lung cancer, smoking typically starts before the diagnosis of lung cancer, often years or even decades earlier.

5. **Reversibility and Prevention**: Reducing or eliminating the cause (smoking) leads to a decrease in the effect (lung cancer incidence). Public health campaigns and smoking cessation programs have been shown to reduce lung cancer rates over time, supporting the idea that smoking is a cause of lung cancer.

6. **Comparison of the Options**:
   - A. Lung Cancer causes Smoking: This option suggests that having lung cancer would lead to smoking, which does not align with the established medical understanding or the temporal relationship required for causality.
   - B. Smoking causes Lung Cancer: This option aligns with the medical evidence, causal mechanism, temporal relationship, and the impact of prevention and reversibility.

Based on the analysis, it's clear that the relationship where smoking leads to lung cancer is supported by extensive medical research, logical temporal relationships, and the understanding of how carcinogens in tobacco smoke can lead to cancer.

<Answer>B</Answer>
