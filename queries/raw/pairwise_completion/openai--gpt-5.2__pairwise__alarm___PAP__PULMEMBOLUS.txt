Let’s define the terms and then check the physiological direction of causality.

### 1) What is PAP?
**PAP = pulmonary artery pressure** (pressure in the pulmonary arterial circulation). It’s a hemodynamic *measurement/outcome* reflecting pulmonary vascular resistance, flow, and left-sided filling pressures (depending on context).

### 2) What is a pulmonary embolus (PULMEMBOLUS)?
A **pulmonary embolus** is an obstruction (usually a thrombus) in the pulmonary arterial tree, which **acutely reduces the effective cross-sectional area** of the pulmonary vasculature.

### 3) What happens to pressures when you obstruct the pulmonary arterial bed?
An acute obstruction leads to:
- **↑ pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR)** distal/proximal to obstruction
- Right ventricle must generate higher pressure to maintain flow
- Result: **↑ PAP** (often abrupt), potentially with RV strain/dilation and reduced cardiac output

So the typical causal chain is:
**Pulmonary embolus → increased PVR → increased PAP (and RV strain).**

### 4) Could high PAP cause a pulmonary embolus?
Chronically elevated PAP (pulmonary hypertension) can predispose to in situ thrombosis in some pulmonary hypertension subtypes, but in ICU monitoring context and general clinical causality, **PAP is not the usual primary cause of an acute pulmonary embolus** (which most commonly originates from venous thromboembolism—DVT → embolization).

Thus, **B** is the more likely and clinically standard cause-effect direction.
