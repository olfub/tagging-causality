1. **Define the variables**
   - **CATECHOL**: circulating catecholamines (e.g., epinephrine/norepinephrine), whether endogenous (stress response) or exogenous (vasopressors/inotropes).
   - **HR**: heart rate.

2. **Physiologic mechanism from CATECHOL → HR**
   - Catecholamines stimulate **β1-adrenergic receptors** in the sinoatrial node and myocardium.
   - This increases **chronotropy** (HR), **dromotropy**, and contractility.
   - In ICU practice, starting/increasing catecholaminergic agents (e.g., epinephrine, dopamine, dobutamine, norepinephrine to a lesser extent) commonly leads to **tachycardia**.

3. **Consider the reverse direction (HR → CATECHOL)**
   - HR changes can be associated with changes in sympathetic tone via **baroreflex/autonomic reflexes**, but HR is typically an **output/marker** of autonomic activity rather than a primary driver of catecholamine release.
   - When HR rises, it is usually because catecholamines (or other causes like fever, pain, hypovolemia, arrhythmia) are already elevated—not because the higher HR itself directly causes catecholamine secretion.
   - There are indirect loops (e.g., hypotension → sympathetic activation → catecholamines → HR), but that is **not** a direct “HR causes catecholamines” relationship.

4. **Most likely direct cause-and-effect**
   - The most direct and physiologically grounded direction is **catecholamines increasing HR**.
