Conversely, individuals with lower socioeconomic status may be constrained to older, less expensive used vehicles (lower `VehicleYear`).
*   **Plausibility:** This aligns with standard economic theory and consumer behavior. Financial resources precede the acquisition of durable goods.

*   **Mechanism:** This would imply that owning a newer car directly increases a person's income, education, or social class.
*   **Plausibility:** This is highly implausible. While reliable transportation can help maintain employment, the specific model year of a car does not generate income or determine social status. Buying a newer car is a consumption decision based on existing resources, not a production function that creates those resources.

**Step 4: Apply the Intervention Test**
*   If we were to intervene and increase a person's income (`SocioEcon`), they would likely upgrade to a newer car (`VehicleYear` changes).
*   If we were to intervene and change the car they drive to a newer model (`VehicleYear`), their income or education level (`SocioEcon`) would not change as a result.

**Step 5: Conclusion for Risk Modeling**
*   In insurance risk modeling, it is crucial to identify exogenous variables (drivers) versus endogenous variables (outcomes). Socioeconomic factors are fundamental drivers that influence vehicle choice, not the other way around. Modeling `VehicleYear` as a cause of `SocioEcon` would introduce reverse causality bias.
