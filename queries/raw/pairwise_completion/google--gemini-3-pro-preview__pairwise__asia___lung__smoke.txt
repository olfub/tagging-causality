To determine the correct cause-and-effect relationship, we need to analyze the biological and temporal evidence associated with smoking and lung cancer.

**Step 1: Analyze the Temporal Sequence**
*   **Scenario A (Lung Cancer causes Smoking):** For this to be true, a person would first develop lung cancer and then, as a direct result of the disease, start smoking. While a diagnosis might cause behavioral changes, there is no biological mechanism by which the growth of a tumor compels a person to inhale tobacco smoke. Furthermore, the vast majority of smokers start smoking years or decades before any cancer diagnosis.
*   **Scenario B (Smoking causes Lung Cancer):** In this scenario, a person smokes for a period of time, and subsequently, lung cancer develops. This aligns with the observed timeline in patients: exposure to the carcinogens precedes the development of the disease.

**Step 2: Analyze Biological Mechanisms**
*   **Smoking:** Tobacco smoke contains over 7,000 chemicals, at least 69 of which are known carcinogens (cancer-causing agents) such as benzene, polonium-210, and benzo[a]pyrene. When inhaled, these chemicals damage the DNA in the cells lining the lungs.
*   **Lung Cancer:** Cancer occurs when DNA damage causes cells to mutate and grow uncontrollably.
*   **Connection:** There is a well-established biological pathway where the carcinogens in smoke directly damage lung cell DNA, leading to mutations that result in cancer.

**Step 3: Review Medical Consensus and Statistical Evidence**
*   Decades of epidemiological studies have shown a strong dose-response relationship: the more a person smokes, the higher their risk of developing lung cancer.
*   The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) states that cigarette smoking is the number one risk factor for lung cancer, linked to about 80% to 90% of lung cancer deaths in the United States.

**Conclusion**
The evidence overwhelmingly supports that the inhalation of tobacco smoke leads to cellular damage and genetic mutations that result in lung cancer. The reverse (lung cancer causing a person to smoke) has no biological basis.

Therefore, the correct relationship is that smoking causes lung cancer.
