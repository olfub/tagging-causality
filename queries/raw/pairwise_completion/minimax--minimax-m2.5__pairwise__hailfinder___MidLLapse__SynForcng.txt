**Step‑by‑step reasoning**

1. **Definitions**  
   - *MidLLapse* (mid‑level lapse rate): the rate of temperature decrease with height in the middle troposphere. A steeper lapse rate indicates greater static instability.  
   - *SynForcng* (synoptic forcing): large‑scale dynamic lift (e.g., fronts, troughs, jet streaks) that promotes ascent and cloud formation.

2. **Typical physical chain**  
   - Synoptic forcing (e.g., an approaching front or upper‑level trough) provides broad, sustained upward motion.  
   - This upward motion causes adiabatic cooling aloft, which generally steepens the mid‑level temperature gradient (i.e., increases the mid‑level lapse rate).  
   - The resulting steeper lapse rate then contributes to convective instability favorable for hail, but it is a *result* of the synoptic‑scale uplift, not its cause.

3. **Causal direction in forecasting**  
   - Forecasters look at synoptic patterns first to anticipate where large‑scale lift will occur.  
   - They then examine the mid‑level lapse rate to assess the magnitude of potential instability.  
   - This workflow reflects the accepted causality: **SynForcng → MidLLapse**.

   - While a steep mid‑level lapse can enhance convection, it does not itself generate the large‑scale synoptic forcing.  
   - Feedbacks from deep convection can influence the synoptic flow, but these are secondary and not the primary driver in typical hail‑forecasting contexts.

**Conclusion:** The cause‑and‑effect relationship that is more likely and operationally recognized is that synoptic forcing leads to changes in the mid‑level lapse rate.
