**Step‑by‑step reasoning**

1. **What is dewpoint?**  
   Dewpoint temperature is a measure of the amount of moisture in the lower atmosphere (the air near the surface). Higher dewpoints mean more moisture.

2. **What is “WindAloft”?**  
   “WindAloft” refers to wind patterns at higher altitudes (e.g., low‑level jets, upper‑level winds). These winds can transport large masses of air over long distances.

3. **Typical meteorological relationships**  
   - **Low‑level jets** (a component of WindAloft) often carry moist air from oceanic source regions (e.g., the Gulf of Mexico) into a region. This advection of moist air raises surface dewpoints.  
   - Conversely, the mere presence of high dewpoints at the surface does not directly generate or change the speed/direction of winds several thousand feet aloft. While high dewpoints can influence local stability and convective processes, they are not a primary driver of the larger‑scale wind field aloft.

4. **Which direction is more plausible as a cause‑and‑effect?**  
   The evidence points to **WindAloft → Dewpoints**. The advection of moisture by upper‑level winds (especially low‑level jets) is a well‑established mechanism that raises surface dewpoints. The reverse (dewpoints causing wind aloft) is far less common.

Therefore, the more likely causal relationship is **B. WindAloft causes Dewpoints**.

<Answer>B</Answer>
