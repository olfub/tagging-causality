To determine the more likely cause-and-effect relationship between "Cancer" and "Smoker," let's analyze the situation step by step:

1. **Definition of Terms**:
   - **Smoker**: An individual who smokes tobacco or other substances.
   - **Cancer**: A disease characterized by the uncontrolled growth and spread of abnormal cells. In this context, we're focusing on lung cancer.

2. **Establishing Causality**:
   - Causality implies that one event (the cause) leads to another event (the effect). For a relationship to be causal, the cause must precede the effect in time, and there must be a plausible mechanism by which the cause can lead to the effect.

3. **Analyzing Direction of Causality**:
   - **A. Cancer causes Smoker**: This would imply that having cancer leads to becoming a smoker. While it's possible that someone might start smoking after being diagnosed with cancer (perhaps due to stress or as part of a larger set of behavioral responses to a diagnosis), this is not the primary or most likely direction of causality. Cancer diagnosis does not typically cause someone to start smoking.
   - **B. Smoker causes Cancer**: This suggests that being a smoker leads to an increased risk of developing cancer, specifically lung cancer. A vast amount of scientific evidence supports this direction of causality. Smoking is a well-established risk factor for lung cancer, with chemicals in tobacco smoke damaging lung cells' DNA, leading to cancer.

4. **Conclusion**:
   - Based on the analysis, the relationship where **Smoker causes Cancer** is more plausible and supported by extensive scientific evidence. Smoking is a major risk factor for lung cancer, and the mechanism by which smoking leads to cancer (through the inhalation of carcinogens that damage lung cells) is well understood.

<Answer>B</Answer>
