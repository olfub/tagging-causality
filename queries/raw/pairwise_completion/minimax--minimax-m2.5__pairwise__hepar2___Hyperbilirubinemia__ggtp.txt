**Step‑by‑step reasoning**

1. **Physiological roles**  
   - *Hyperbilirubinemia* is an elevated level of bilirubin in the blood, usually due to increased production (e.g., hemolysis) or decreased excretion (e.g., cholestasis, liver disease).  
   - *GGT* (γ‑glutamyl transpeptidase) is an enzyme located mainly on the membrane of biliary epithelial cells. Its serum activity rises when the biliary tree is injured or when cholestasis is present.

2. **Typical laboratory patterns**  
   - In *hemolytic* (pre‑hepatic) jaundice, bilirubin is high but liver enzymes such as GGT are usually normal.  
   - In *cholestatic* (obstructive) jaundice, both conjugated bilirubin **and** GGT are elevated because the same disease process (biliary obstruction or hepatic cholestasis) leads to impaired bile flow and leakage/induction of GGT.

3. **Causal relationships**  
   - Neither elevated bilirubin nor elevated GGT directly causes the other. Both are downstream **effects** of an underlying pathological process (e.g., biliary obstruction, hepatitis, drug‑induced cholestasis).  
   - However, when one must choose between the two proposed directed relationships, the scenario “hyperbilirubinemia → GGT elevation” is slightly more plausible than the reverse, because severe cholestasis that raises bilirubin can also cause cellular stress that modestly raises GGT. The opposite direction (GGT causing bilirubin rise) has no physiological basis.

4. **Conclusion**  
   - The most plausible (though still not truly causal) relationship among the two options is that hyperbilirubinemia can be associated with a secondary rise in GGT, making option **A** the better choice.
