We need to determine which direct cause-and-effect relationship between JohnCalls and MaryCalls is more plausible, given our knowledge of the earthquake domain. Let's analyze step by step.

### Step 1: Understand the Domain
In the classic earthquake/alarm scenario, an earthquake (or burglary) can trigger an alarm. John and Mary are neighbors who call a homeowner when they hear the alarm. The true causal structure is:

- Earthquake/Burglary → Alarm → {JohnCalls, MaryCalls}.

Thus, JohnCalls and MaryCalls are both effects of the alarm; there is no direct causal link between them. They become correlated because they share a common cause (the alarm), but neither one causes the other.

### Step 2: Why the Question Asks for a Choice
The question forces a binary choice between A and B, implying we must evaluate which direction would be more plausible if we were to (incorrectly) assume a direct causal link. We need to use domain knowledge and plausible reasoning to decide.

### Step 3: Statistical Symmetry
From a purely statistical viewpoint, given only the two variables, both directional models can perfectly fit the observed joint distribution. Maximum likelihood would not favor one over the other. Therefore, we must bring in additional considerations.

### Step 4: Use Domain‑Specific Knowledge
We know the typical parameters from the alarm network:

- P(JohnCalls | Alarm) = 0.90, P(JohnCalls | ¬Alarm) = 0.05  
- P(MaryCalls | Alarm) = 0.70, P(MaryCalls | ¬Alarm) = 0.01  
- P(Alarm) is very low (~0.0025).

From these we can compute approximate marginal probabilities:

- P(JohnCalls) ≈ 0.052  
- P(MaryCalls) ≈ 0.012

John calls more often than Mary, both because he is more sensitive to the alarm and has a higher false‑positive rate.

### Step 5: Entropy as a Heuristic for Causality
In causal discovery from two variables, a common heuristic is that the **cause** tends to have higher entropy (greater uncertainty) than the effect. The reasoning is that the effect is often a (noisy) function of the cause, leading to a more skewed distribution. Here:

- Entropy of JohnCalls ≈ 0.29 bits  
- Entropy of MaryCalls ≈ 0.09 bits

JohnCalls has higher entropy, suggesting it is more likely to be the cause and MaryCalls the effect.

### Step 6: Timing Considerations
If the alarm sounds, John has a higher probability of calling (0.90 vs. 0.70). In a race to call first, John is more likely to be the earlier caller. If one were to (mistakenly) interpret a temporal order as evidence of causation, John calling before Mary would support the direction JohnCalls → MaryCalls.

### Step 7: Direct Influence Plausibility
While both directions are false in the true model, the idea that John’s call could prompt Mary to call is slightly more plausible if we imagine a scenario where John might contact Mary (though the standard story does not include that). Mary’s call being the cause would require that Mary’s call directly influences John’s decision, which is less intuitive because John already has a high independent propensity to call when the alarm sounds.

### Conclusion
Considering entropy, marginal frequencies, and timing heuristics, the direction **JohnCalls causes MaryCalls** is more plausible (though still incorrect compared to the true common‑cause structure). Therefore, we choose option A.
