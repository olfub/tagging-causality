To determine the cause-and-effect relationship between Central Venous Pressure (CVP) and Left Ventricular End-Diastolic Volume (LVEDVOLUME), we should understand what each term represents and how they are physiologically related.

1. **Central Venous Pressure (CVP)**: CVP is the pressure within the superior and inferior vena cava, which are the two large veins returning deoxygenated blood from the body to the heart. It is an indicator of the blood's pressure in the right atrium and, by extension, can reflect the right ventricle's preload (the initial stretching of the cardiac myocytes prior to contraction). CVP is influenced by factors such as blood volume, venous tone, and right heart function.

2. **Left Ventricular End-Diastolic Volume (LVEDVOLUME)**: This refers to the volume of blood in the left ventricle at the end of diastole (the relaxation phase of the heart), just before the ventricle starts to contract. It is an indicator of the left ventricle's preload. LVEDVOLUME is critical because it influences the stroke volume (the amount of blood pumped by the left ventricle of the heart in one contraction) through the Frank-Starling mechanism, where increased preload (and thus LVEDVOLUME) leads to increased stroke volume.

Considering the physiological relationships:

- **CVP Causes LVEDVOLUME (Option A)**: This would imply that the pressure in the central veins directly causes a change in the volume of blood in the left ventricle at the end of diastole. While CVP can reflect overall volume status and indirectly influence left ventricular filling through its impact on right heart function and subsequent pulmonary circulation, it is not a direct cause of LVEDVOLUME.

- **LVEDVOLUME Causes CVP (Option B)**: This suggests that the volume of blood in the left ventricle at the end of diastole influences the pressure in the central veins. In reality, the direct influence is more from the overall volume status and right heart function on CVP rather than LVEDVOLUME directly affecting CVP. However, in a broader sense, an increase in LVEDVOLUME might indicate an increase in overall preload, which can be associated with an increase in CVP if the right ventricle is also experiencing increased preload. But this relationship is more indirect and nuanced.

Given these considerations, the direct causal relationship as described in both options might not fully capture the physiological complexity.
