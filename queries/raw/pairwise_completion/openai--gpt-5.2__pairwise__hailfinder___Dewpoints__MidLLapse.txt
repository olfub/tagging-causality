1. **Define the variables**
   - **Dewpoints** (typically near-surface dewpoint) primarily reflect **low-level moisture content**, controlled by **moisture advection**, surface fluxes, and boundary-layer mixing.
   - **MidLLapse** (mid-level lapse rate, e.g., 700–500 mb) reflects the **mid-tropospheric temperature structure**, controlled by **warm/cold advection aloft**, **subsidence**, and features like an **elevated mixed layer (EML)**.

2. **Ask what physically drives what**
   - Changing **dewpoint** changes the amount of water vapor in the low levels, but it does **not directly set** the mid-level temperature gradient. Moisture can influence lapse rates *after saturation and latent heating*, but that’s mainly about **parcel ascent/instability (CAPE)** and the **moist adiabatic lapse rate in saturated ascent**, not usually the **environmental mid-level lapse rate** itself.
   - Changing **mid-level lapse rate** often comes with a different **airmass aloft** (e.g., EML, dry intrusions). That can change boundary-layer evolution: **steeper lapse rates encourage deeper mixing**, which can **mix down drier air and lower surface dewpoints** (or at least modulate them).

3. **Consider the common forecast situation**
   - In severe/hail setups, you often see **steep mid-level lapse rates (EML)** overlying **high low-level dewpoints**. The co-occurrence is usually because **separate processes** are happening (EML advected in aloft; moisture advected in at low levels), not because dewpoints “cause” the mid-level lapse rate.

4. **Direction that makes more causal sense**
   - **MidLLapse → Dewpoints** is more plausible (via mixing/entrainment impacts on surface moisture), whereas **Dewpoints → MidLLapse** is generally not a primary physical control on the environmental mid-level lapse rate.
