This is a classic problem often used in Bayesian networks and causal modeling (specifically the "Earthquake" or "Burglary" network examples). Let's reason through this step by step.

**Step 1: Define the variables in a typical context.**
*   **Alarm:** This usually refers to a home burglar alarm or earthquake warning alarm going off.
*   **MaryCalls:** This refers to a neighbor named Mary calling you on the telephone.

**Step 2: Analyze the temporal and logical sequence.**
*   **Scenario A (Alarm $\rightarrow$ MaryCalls):**
    1.  An event (like an earthquake or burglary) triggers the alarm.
    2.  The alarm makes a loud noise.
    3.  Mary, the neighbor, hears the loud noise.
    4.  Driven by hearing the noise, Mary picks up the phone to call you and let you know your alarm is ringing.
    *   *Plausibility:* High. This follows a logical flow of cause (stimulus) leading to effect (reaction).

*   **Scenario B (MaryCalls $\rightarrow$ Alarm):**
    1.  Mary decides to call you.
    2.  The act of Mary calling somehow triggers your home alarm mechanism to start ringing.
    *   *Plausibility:* Low. Unless the system is wired incredibly strangely (e.g., the phone ringing creates a vibration that trips a sensitive sensor, or the phone system is wired into the alarm trigger), a phone call does not typically cause a security or earthquake alarm to activate.

**Step 3: Evaluate Causal Direction.**
In causal modeling, we look for which event provides the mechanism for the other to happen.
*   The alarm provides the *information* or *auditory signal* that motivates Mary to act.
*   Mary's action (calling) is a response to that signal.
*   Therefore, the state of the Alarm influences the probability of Mary calling.

**Step 4: Conclusion.**
It is standard causal logic that the alarm sounding is the cause that leads to the effect of the neighbor calling to report it.
