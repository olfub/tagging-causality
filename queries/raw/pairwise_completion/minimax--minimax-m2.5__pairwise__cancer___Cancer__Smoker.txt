**Step‑by‑step reasoning**

1. **Temporality**: To infer causality, the presumed cause must precede the effect. Lung cancer is diagnosed after a person has been smoking for some time; the onset of cancer occurs later than the habit of smoking.
2. **Strength of association**: Epidemiology consistently shows a strong dose‑response relationship: the more a person smokes (higher pack‑years), the greater the risk of developing lung cancer.
3. **Biological plausibility**: Cigarette smoke contains carcinogens (e.g., benzo[a]pyrene, nitrosamines) that cause DNA damage in lung tissue, leading to malignant transformation.
4. **Consistency**: The link between smoking and lung cancer is observed across many populations, study designs, and time periods.
5. **Alternative explanations**: While other factors (e.g., asbestos exposure, genetic predisposition) can also influence lung cancer risk, they do not explain the high incidence of lung cancer in smokers relative to non‑smokers.

Given the above evidence, the direction of causality is clear: **being a smoker increases the risk of developing lung cancer**. Therefore, the cause‑and‑effect relationship “Smoker causes Cancer” (option B) is the one supported by scientific evidence.
