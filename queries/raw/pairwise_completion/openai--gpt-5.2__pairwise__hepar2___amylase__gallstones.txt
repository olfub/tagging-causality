1. **Clarify what “amylase” represents clinically**  
   - Serum **amylase** is an enzyme (largely from the pancreas and salivary glands).  
   - In practice, “amylase” is often used as shorthand for **elevated serum amylase**, which is a **marker** of pancreatic inflammation or injury (e.g., pancreatitis), not typically a causative agent of unrelated conditions.

2. **Evaluate whether amylase could plausibly cause gallstones (A)**  
   - **Gallstones** form in the gallbladder from precipitation of bile components (cholesterol stones, pigment stones), driven by factors like bile supersaturation, gallbladder stasis, and nucleation factors.  
   - Amylase does **not** participate in bile chemistry in a way that would generate gallstones, and there’s no standard pathophysiologic mechanism where amylase *causes* gallstone formation.  
   → So **A is very unlikely**.

3. **Evaluate whether gallstones could plausibly cause elevated amylase (B)**  
   - Gallstones can obstruct the **ampulla of Vater** or the distal common bile duct.  
   - This can trigger **gallstone pancreatitis** via transient obstruction and reflux/ductal hypertension, leading to pancreatic inflammation.  
   - Pancreatic inflammation commonly causes **elevated serum amylase** (and lipase).  
   → So **B is clinically common and mechanistically well-supported**.

4. **Conclusion**  
   - The likely causal direction is **gallstones → pancreatitis → elevated amylase**, not the reverse.

<Answer>B</Answer>
