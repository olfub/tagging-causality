To determine the correct cause-and-effect relationship, we need to analyze the physiology of the cardiovascular system, specifically the direction of blood flow and pressure transmission.

**Step 1: Define the Variables**
*   **CVP (Central Venous Pressure):** The pressure in the thoracic vena cava, near the right atrium. It represents the filling pressure for the right ventricle.
*   **LVEDVOLUME (Left Ventricular End-Diastolic Volume):** The volume of blood in the left ventricle at the end of filling (diastole). It represents the preload for the left ventricle.

**Step 2: Analyze the "Forward" Flow Hypothesis (Option A: CVP $\rightarrow$ LVEDVOLUME)**
Blood flows from the systemic circulation $\rightarrow$ Right Atrium (CVP) $\rightarrow$ Right Ventricle $\rightarrow$ Lungs $\rightarrow$ Left Atrium $\rightarrow$ Left Ventricle (LVEDVOLUME).
One might argue that a higher CVP (indicating more volume returning to the heart) could lead to increased flow through the system, eventually resulting in a higher LVEDVOLUME. While this "forward transmission" of volume is possible in cases of hypervolemia, it is not a deterministic causal rule because high CVP often results from Right Heart Failure. In Right Heart Failure, CVP is high, but the right ventricle cannot pump effectively, leading to *low* forward flow. Consequently, the Left Ventricle receives less blood, and LVEDVOLUME would be low. Therefore, high CVP does not consistently cause high LVEDVOLUME.

**Step 3: Analyze the "Backward" Pressure Transmission Hypothesis (Option B: LVEDVOLUME $\rightarrow$ CVP)**
Let's consider the relationship based on pressure transmission backward through the circulation.
If the Left Ventricle has a high End-Diastolic Volume (LVEDVOLUME), it often implies either volume overload or, more critically, Left Ventricular Failure (where the ventricle is dilated and cannot pump effectively).
1.  Increased LVEDVOLUME generally leads to increased Left Ventricular End-Diastolic Pressure (LVEDP).
2.  This increased pressure transmits backward to the Left Atrium $\rightarrow$ Pulmonary Veins $\rightarrow$ Pulmonary Capillaries $\rightarrow$ Pulmonary Artery $\rightarrow$ Right Ventricle $\rightarrow$ Right Atrium (CVP).
This phenomenon is known as "backward failure." An increase in left-sided pressures (driven by LVEDV/LVEDP) creates a back-pressure that the right heart must pump against. This inevitably leads to an increase in CVP.

**Step 4: Comparison and Conclusion**
The relationship B (LVEDVOLUME $\rightarrow$ CVP) represents a fundamental physiological mechanism where the status of the left ventricle dictates the load on the right heart through retrograde pressure transmission. In clinical practice, a rising CVP is often a late sign of left-sided heart issues (high LVEDV/failure).

Conversely, relationship A (CVP $\rightarrow$ LVEDVOLUME) is inconsistent. As noted in Step 2, a patient can have a very high CVP due to Right Heart Failure while having a dangerously low LVEDVOLUME because the blood is trapped in the venous system and not reaching the left heart.

Therefore, the causal direction where Left Ventricular End-Diastolic Volume influences Central Venous Pressure (via backward transmission) is the physiologically dominant and consistent relationship.

<Answer>B</Answer>
