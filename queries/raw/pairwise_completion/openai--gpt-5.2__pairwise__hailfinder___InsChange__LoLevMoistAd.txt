Let’s interpret the variables in a hail-forecasting/convective environment context:

- **LoLevMoistAd** = low-level moisture advection (transport of moist air by the wind in the lowest layers, e.g., 925–850 mb), typically driven by the pressure/wind field (LLJ, warm advection regime, etc.).
- **InsChange** = change in insolation (incoming solar radiation at the surface), most often controlled by cloud cover/optical depth, sun angle/diurnal cycle, and to a lesser extent aerosols.

### Step 1: Ask which one is more “dynamically upstream”
Low-level moisture advection is fundamentally a **mass/wind-transport** process set by synoptic/mesoscale pressure gradients and boundary-layer wind structure (e.g., LLJ). Insolation changes do **not** usually determine whether moist air is being advected into a region; winds do.

So it’s unlikely that **InsChange** is a primary cause of **LoLevMoistAd**.

### Step 2: Check the plausible pathway from moisture advection → insolation change
When moist air advects into an area, it commonly increases:
- boundary-layer humidity,
- low-level saturation potential,
- **stratus/fog/low clouds** development (especially overnight/morning),
- and sometimes deeper cloudiness as convection initiates.

More low clouds/deeper cloud cover directly reduces surface solar radiation, producing a clear mechanism:
**LoLevMoistAd → more clouds → InsChange (typically decreased insolation).**

### Step 3: Consider the reverse pathway (insolation change → moisture advection)
Changes in insolation can modify:
- surface heating,
- boundary-layer mixing depth,
- local circulations (sea breeze, slope flows),
- and surface moisture *availability* via evapotranspiration.

But these effects more strongly impact **mixing/vertical redistribution of moisture** (e.g., dewpoint changes by mixing) rather than causing **horizontal moisture advection** itself. Moisture advection is mainly controlled by the low-level wind field; insolation changes are usually too indirect to be the “cause” of advection.

### Step 4: Decide which direction is more likely in hail forecasting practice
In forecasting severe convection/hail, low-level moisture return/advection often precedes and helps produce cloud changes (morning stratus, reduced heating, differential clearing). Forecasters frequently reason from moisture advection to cloud/insolation impacts, not the other way around.

**Therefore, the more likely causal relationship is: LoLevMoistAd causes InsChange.**
