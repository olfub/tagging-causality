Here is the step-by-step reasoning to determine the correct cause-and-effect relationship:

1.  **Define the Terms:**
    *   **Anaphylaxis:** A severe, potentially life-threatening systemic allergic reaction. It involves the rapid release of inflammatory mediators (such as histamine) from mast cells and basophils.
    *   **TPR:** In the context of an Intensive Care Unit (ICU) and hemodynamic monitoring, TPR typically stands for **Total Peripheral Resistance** (also known as Systemic Vascular Resistance). While TPR can also stand for Temperature, Pulse, and Respiration in general nursing, the pairing with a shock state like anaphylaxis strongly implies the hemodynamic parameter.

2.  **Analyze the Pathophysiology:**
    *   During anaphylaxis, the release of inflammatory mediators causes widespread **vasodilation** (the widening of blood vessels).
    *   Vasodilation reduces the tone of the smooth muscle in the vessel walls.
    *   This reduction in vascular tone directly leads to a decrease in the resistance to blood flow throughout the systemic circulation.

3.  **Establish the Causal Link:**
    *   Because the vasodilation is a direct result of the anaphylactic reaction, the **Anaphylaxis** is the initiating event (the cause).
    *   The resulting decrease in vascular resistance is the **TPR** change (the effect).
    *   Therefore, the physiological cascade flows from the allergic reaction to the hemodynamic change.

4.  **Evaluate the Reverse:**
    *   It is not physiologically plausible for a change in Total Peripheral Resistance (TPR) to trigger the immunological cascade required to cause Anaphylaxis. Low resistance is a consequence of the shock state, not the trigger of the allergy.

5.  **Conclusion:**
    *   Anaphylaxis leads to vasodilation, which lowers Total Peripheral Resistance. Thus, Anaphylaxis causes the change in TPR.
