To determine the more likely cause-and-effect relationship, let's analyze the nature of the variables and how they interact in a demographic context.

**Step 1: Define the Variables**
*   **Occupation:** This refers to a person's job or profession. It is a key socioeconomic indicator that determines income level, social status, daily schedule, and available leisure time. It is generally a relatively stable characteristic.
*   **Travel:** This refers to the movement of people between locations. In demographics, this usually encompasses business trips, commuting, or leisure tourism. It is often a behavior or activity rather than a static status.

**Step 2: Analyze "Occupation causes Travel" (Option A)**
*   **Job Requirements:** Many occupations explicitly require travel as part of the job duties (e.g., pilots, sales representatives, consultants, truck drivers, diplomats). In these cases, the occupation is the direct cause of the travel.
*   **Financial Means:** Occupation determines income. Higher-income occupations provide the financial resources necessary for leisure travel.
*   **Time Availability:** Different occupations come with different leave policies and schedules (e.g., teachers having summer breaks vs. retail workers having irregular shifts). This directly dictates when and how much a person can travel.
*   **Conclusion for A:** There is a strong, structural mechanism where occupation dictates the necessity, ability, and frequency of travel.

**Step 3: Analyze "Travel causes Occupation" (Option B)**
*   **Career Inspiration:** It is possible for someone to travel, enjoy the experience, and decide to pursue a career in the travel industry (e.g., becoming a travel agent or tour guide).
*   **Relocation:** Sometimes people travel to a new location and find employment there. However, this is often categorized more as migration driven by labor markets rather than "travel" causing the occupation itself.
*   **Frequency:** For the vast majority of the population, travel does not determine their profession. People usually choose occupations based on education, skills, and local market demand, not primarily based on the act of traveling.
*   **Conclusion for B:** While possible in specific individual cases, this is not a general demographic rule. The causal link is weak compared to the reverse.

**Step 4: Compare the Strength of the Relationships**
In demographic and socioeconomic modeling, occupation is typically treated as an independent variable (a predictor) that influences lifestyle behaviors (dependent variables), such as spending habits, housing, and travel. The resources and constraints provided by an occupation are fundamental drivers of travel behavior. Conversely, travel is rarely the primary driver of occupational status for the general population.

**Step 5: Final Determination**
Because occupation dictates income, time, and job-related movement requirements, it is the primary driver of travel behavior. Therefore, the relationship where Occupation causes Travel is significantly more likely and robust.

<Answer>A</Answer>
