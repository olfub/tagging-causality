Based on general medical knowledge and established causal networks for liver disorders (often represented in Bayesian networks like the HEPAR II model), let's analyze the causal impact of **alcoholism**:

1.  **Steatosis (Fatty Liver):** Chronic alcohol consumption is a primary cause of hepatic steatosis. Alcohol metabolism leads to the accumulation of triglycerides in liver cells. Thus, *alcoholism -> steatosis*.
2.  **THepatitis (Toxic Hepatitis/Alcoholic Hepatitis):** Excessive alcohol intake causes direct toxicity to liver cells, leading to inflammation known as alcoholic hepatitis. Thus, *alcoholism -> thepatitis*.
3.  **Cirrhosis:** Long-term alcoholism is a leading cause of liver cirrhosis (scarring), typically progressing from steatosis and hepatitis. Thus, *alcoholism -> cirrhosis*.
4.  **Social/Behavioral History (Alcohol):** In medical datasets, the variable measuring current alcohol intake or history is often directly determined by the condition of alcoholism. Thus, *alcoholism -> alcohol*.

Other variables listed (like `ascites`, `hepatomegaly`, `alt`, `ast`) are usually secondary effects caused by the liver conditions (Steatosis, Hepatitis, Cirrhosis) rather than being *direct* children of the "alcoholism" variable in a structured causal graph. For example, alcoholism causes cirrhosis, and cirrhosis causes ascites.

Therefore, the direct downstream effects are usually the varying stages of alcoholic liver disease and the measurement of intake itself.
